What the system actually does

Four beats, and none of them are complicated. A camera watches a zone. A fast model asks one narrow question. A second model draws the answer. The frame goes to the people who can walk over and look.

ONE

Watch the zone

Your existing cameras, or a handful of cheap ones. Rooflines, gates, the outside of the perimeter — the places a person can be and nobody can see.

TWO

Ask one narrow question

A small vision model watches for one thing: a person where a person shouldn't be. Narrow questions are fast — about 1.3 seconds on a low-resolution frame.

THREE

Draw the shape

A second model outlines exactly what it found and labels it with a confidence. The field team sees a person, not a blinking dot on a map.

FOUR

Push it out

The annotated frame arrives on the phones of the people already standing on the ground. They walk over and look. That is the whole loop.

Why roofs

Butler, Pennsylvania — 13 July 2024

On 13 July 2024, a shooter climbed onto the roof of the AGR International complex near the Butler Farm Show grounds and fired at a former president from a position roughly 400–450 feet from the stage. The public record of how that happened is a list of things a persistent, boring camera could have made visible.

  1. The building housed three police counter-snipers assigned to cover the event. None of them were on the roof, because of a staffing shortage.
  2. The slant of that roof may have hidden the shooter from Secret Service snipers; a tree line blocked the northern team's view.
  3. Bystanders saw a man on the roof and reported him to police minutes before the shots.
  4. The independent reviews that followed called it the agency's most significant security failure since 1981.

None of those failures were caused by a lack of people or a lack of cameras. They were caused by a roof that nobody could see continuously, and by a report that had to travel by radio through a chain of people.

VisionBridge is not a replacement for a counter-sniper team or a radio net. It is the cheap, tireless layer underneath them: it looks at the same roofs, every second, and the moment a shape appears on one, the picture is already on the phones of the people who can walk over and check.

Sources: public reporting on the 13 July 2024 shooting, incl. the Senate Homeland Security & Governmental Affairs review and contemporaneous investigative reporting. Facts stated as reported; figures in the table above measured on our own hardware, 15 Sept 2026.

What the field team gets

  • An alert with the frame attached — the actual image, already masked, with confidence and the zone name.
  • No login ceremony. The alert goes to phones that are already on site, on your own link.
  • One tap opens the mask and the zone view, so the responder can see the angle before they walk.
  • Every alert is logged with a timestamp and the frame, so the day can be replayed afterwards.

What it takes to stand it up

hardware
One on-site machine — an Apple Silicon box or a small edge unit. No GPU cluster, no racks.
cameras
Anything that already streams: existing CCTV, a fixed camera on a pole, a drone feed. We read the stream; we don't replace it.
network
Nothing has to leave the site. Inference runs where the cameras are; alerts travel over the venue's own network.
setup
A camera pointed at the roofline, a zone drawn around it, the field phones added to the alert list. That is the whole configuration.
what it is not
Not a weapons system, not a face-recognition product, and not a replacement for your security plan. It is a persistent pair of eyes on the least-watched surface of your venue.
